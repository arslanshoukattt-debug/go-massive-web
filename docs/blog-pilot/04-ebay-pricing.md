# How to price eBay products using sold listings and your margin floor

**Author: Go Massive**

Set an eBay price by answering two different questions: what have buyers paid for genuinely comparable products, and what must your business retain from the sale? Sold-market evidence informs the first. Your own costs determine the second.

Neither question replaces the other. A competitor’s ambitious asking price does not prove demand. A price that covers your costs does not prove customers will accept it. A workable pricing decision needs both pieces of evidence.

## Build a comparison group you would defend

Start with the exact product, not the broadest description that produces plenty of results. Match the model, condition, quantity, and included components. Consider location and shipping terms where they affect the offer.

eBay’s [Product Research documentation](https://www.ebay.com/help/selling/selling-tools/research?id=4853) describes filters for refining comparisons and access to sold-price information, including actual accepted Best Offer prices. It also distinguishes Product Research from ordinary completed-listing searches.

Use those capabilities to build a relevant group rather than relying on the first average you see. A cheap incomplete item and a complete new unit may share keywords without serving the same buyer.

Keep a short note explaining your exclusions. This lets another person repeat the research and helps you avoid quietly removing inconvenient results simply because they suggest a lower price.

## Compare the offer, not just the headline number

Here is a hypothetical illustration, not current eBay market data. Four sold offers show item prices of $72, $78, $80, and $90. Their buyer-paid shipping charges are $10, $0, $0, and $0.

| Offer | Item price | Shipping paid by buyer | Comparable receipt before tax |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $72 | $10 | $82 |
| B | $78 | $0 | $78 |
| C | $80 | $0 | $80 |
| D | $90 | $0 | $90 |

The lowest item price is not the lowest combined amount. Assuming these are otherwise comparable, the median of the four combined amounts is $81: halfway between $80 and $82.

Four observations would be a thin basis for a pricing policy. The purpose is to show how the comparison changes when you normalize the offer. In real research, record the period, sample size, product differences, and whether unusual sales dominate the result.

## Establish your contribution floor

Now use your own cost information. Consider another hypothetical illustration: you receive $80 including buyer-paid shipping. Your estimated product cost is $38, fulfillment and packaging cost $9, platform and advertising charges $12, and expected return-related cost $3.

That leaves $18 in contribution: $80 minus $62. It is not net profit; fixed overhead remains outside the example. None of those assumed charges is an eBay fee quote.

If you require $15 in contribution, a $3 concession appears available only while the other assumptions hold. Recalculate at the proposed price because some charges may vary with the transaction. Avoid applying a fixed fee assumption to every price automatically.

Include shipping revenue and shipping expense consistently. Also distinguish an expected allowance for returns from an actual refunded sale so you do not count the same loss twice when reconciling results.

## Decide whether the market and the business overlap

If comparable buyers appear willing to pay above your floor, you have a range worth testing. If the apparent market sits below it, lowering the price is not automatically the right answer.

Investigate the mismatch. You might be comparing different conditions, carrying a higher fulfillment cost, or offering a bundle whose value the page does not explain. There may also be no attractive opportunity at the current sourcing cost.

That is useful information. A sale that consistently fails your contribution requirement is not rescued by moving more units, unless you have an explicit, bounded reason to accept the shortfall.

## Test one pricing hypothesis at a time

Choose an initial price and document why it is reasonable. Set a review point based on expected activity rather than changing the offer whenever a quiet day makes you nervous.

- Keep product condition and included components clear.
- Record price, shipping terms, promotions, and major listing edits.
- Compare actual contribution with the estimate after sales occur.
- Investigate low exposure separately from weak purchase response.
- Revisit the comparison group when the market or inventory condition changes.

Your aim is a repeatable pricing process. Evidence should explain both why the offer is credible to a buyer and why the resulting order is worth fulfilling.
